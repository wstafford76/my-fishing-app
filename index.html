Current direction and speed will be identified as modeled or measured. They are not guaranteed to match a specific gut or shoreline seam.

Data sources and limitations

Tide stage and height use NOAA tide predictions from the nearest available tide station. Current speed and direction use Open-Meteo's ocean-current model, which combines currents, waves, and tides at roughly 8 km resolution. Coastal accuracy is limited; use this as general fishing guidance, not navigation data or a measurement at your exact fishing spot.

Understanding Fishing Conditions

Barometric pressure

Air pressure is shown in hectopascals (hPa). Rising means pressure is higher than it was 12 or 24 hours earlier; falling means it is lower. Stable means the change is small. Pressure is only one clue—fish do not respond to a single number in a predictable way.

3-, 12-, and 24-hour pressure changes

These compare the current pressure with readings from those hours earlier. A positive number means pressure rose; a negative number means it fell. The 24-hour comparison gives a broader trend than the 3-hour reading.

Wind speed and direction

Wind can move bait, change surface conditions, and affect casting. In the surf, wind direction relative to the shoreline matters as much as speed. Strong onshore wind often makes the water rougher; sheltered shorelines may fish differently.

Wave height and period

Wave height estimates the vertical size of waves. Wave period is the time between waves. Longer-period waves can carry more energy even when wave height looks similar. These are modeled estimates, not a measurement at your exact cast.

Tides

Incoming tide is water moving toward higher tide; outgoing tide is moving toward lower tide. Current moving through cuts, passes, drains, and channels can concentrate bait and predators. The app uses the nearest available NOAA prediction station, which may not match the exact shoreline.

Water current, flood, ebb, and slack

Current is water moving horizontally. Speed tells you how fast it is moving; direction tells you where it is flowing. NOAA reports tidal-current predictions in knots (1 knot is about 1.15 mph). Flood means the tide-driven current is moving in the local flood direction; ebb means it is moving in the opposite, outgoing direction. Slack is the brief period when the tidal current is weakest as it changes direction. Slack current does not always happen at the exact time of high or low tide. NOAA current predictions are forecasts for a nearby station and depth, not a live measurement at your feet.

Station predictions vs. hydrodynamic model maps

A station prediction describes expected current at one named NOAA station. It does not map the whole beach or pass. A hydrodynamic model such as NOAA NGOFS2 calculates water movement across a wider grid using water levels, tides, winds, and other inputs. NOAA publishes nowcast and short-range forecast guidance for the Galveston Bay region, including San Luis Pass. Model output can reveal broad flow patterns and changes across channels, but it is still guidance: small guts, surf zones, sandbars, and very local eddies may be too small for the model grid. Compare model guidance with what you see on the water.

Measured, modeled, and unverified map information

Measured means a sensor reported data at its gauge or station. Predicted means a station's tide/current forecast. Modeled means a computer model estimates conditions across an area. Unverified means the app has no reliable data for that exact patch of water. A nearby station does not prove the current at your fishing spot, and the app does not draw guessed arrows between stations.

NOAA current station and depth

A NOAA current station measures or predicts water movement at a specific channel or location. The nearest station may be miles away and may be in a pass or ship channel with different flow from your beach. The depth/bin identifies where the current is measured or predicted in the water column; surface and deeper currents can differ. Always compare the station name and distance with your actual fishing spot.

Freshwater flow rate and gauge height

USGS streamflow (discharge) is the volume of water passing a point each second, usually shown in cubic feet per second (cfs). A higher cfs generally means more water moving through that river or creek, but it is not the speed of water at your exact bank spot. Gauge height is the water level at that monitoring station relative to its own reference gauge—not the depth of the lake. Lakes and ponds often have no current gauge; wind, inflowing creeks, dam releases, and outlets can still create localized movement. If no nearby gauge exists, the app will say so rather than invent a current reading.

Best Fishing Windows

The app compares forecast wind, gusts, rain chance, temperature, pressure trend, and—when available—surf waves. Scores rank forecast periods against one another. They are not catch probabilities, and tide timing is not included in the current scoring model.

Fishing Log and condition timeline

Catch entries can save the latest loaded conditions automatically. The Trip Conditions Timeline lets you manually record additional readings during a trip, even when you have not caught a fish. Comparing readings with catches over time may reveal patterns, but small samples can be misleading.
