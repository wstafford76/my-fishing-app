Data Management

Export information, create a complete backup, or import records from another device. Your data is stored locally in this browser unless you export it.

Export Data

Choose the type of information to export. Fishing-log exports include catches, Trip Conditions Timeline readings, and catch photos. Inventory exports contain tackle records. A complete backup includes all supported CastPoint records and photos.

Create Complete Backup

Create one portable file containing your CastPoint records and photos. Keep a copy somewhere outside this device, such as your computer, external drive, or cloud storage.

A full backup includes inventory, gear photos, catches, catch photos, trip condition snapshots, saved fishing locations, and your current freshwater/surf selection.

Import Data / Restore Backup

Fishing-log imports add new catches and condition readings without overwriting existing records. Inventory imports restore inventory data. A full-backup restore replaces the corresponding CastPoint data on this device.

Before restoring a full backup, export anything on this device that you want to keep. Review the confirmation carefully; a full restore replaces existing records rather than merging them.

Backup Information

Full backup contents

Included: tackle inventory and gear photos; catch entries and catch photos; Trip Conditions Timeline snapshots; saved fishing locations; and the current freshwater/surf selection. Live weather, marine forecasts, tide predictions, maps, and other information fetched from online services are not archived as live data. Load conditions again on the new device when you need current readings.

Transfer CastPoint to another device
  1. On the device with your data, open Data Management and select Create Complete Backup.
  2. Save castpoint-full-backup.json somewhere safe outside the device.
  3. Transfer that file to the destination phone or computer.
  4. Open CastPoint on the destination device, go to Data Management, and select Restore full backup.
  5. Choose the JSON file and review the replacement warning before confirming.
  6. Wait for the completion message, then check your inventory, fishing log, photos, and saved locations.
Fishing-log transfer versus full backup

A fishing-log export is smaller and contains catches, Trip Conditions Timeline readings, and catch photos. Importing it adds records that are not already present; matching record IDs are skipped, so importing the same file again will not duplicate records. Existing fishing-log records are never overwritten or deleted.

A full CastPoint backup includes inventory, gear and catch photos, fishing logs, trip conditions, saved locations, and fishing-mode selection. Restoring it replaces the corresponding data on the destination device.

Older version 2 fishing-log exports can still be imported, but they did not include photo files. Full backups and fishing-log exports are different file types; choose the matching import or restore action.

Important backup tips
  • Create a new backup after adding important catches, photos, or inventory items.
  • Keep at least one backup copy somewhere other than the phone running CastPoint.
  • Export destination-device data you want to keep before restoring a full backup.
  • Do not close CastPoint while a restore is running. Wait for the completion message.
  • Backups can be large when they contain many photos. Keep the original backup until you have checked the restored data.

Data sources and limitations

Tide stage and height use NOAA tide predictions from the nearest available tide station. Current speed and direction use Open-Meteo's ocean-current model, which combines currents, waves, and tides at roughly 8 km resolution. Coastal accuracy is limited; use this as general fishing guidance, not navigation data or a measurement at your exact fishing spot.

Understanding Fishing Conditions

Barometric pressure

Air pressure is shown in hectopascals (hPa). Rising means pressure is higher than it was 12 or 24 hours earlier; falling means it is lower. Stable means the change is small. Pressure is only one clue—fish do not respond to a single number in a predictable way.

3-, 12-, and 24-hour pressure changes

These compare the current pressure with readings from those hours earlier. A positive number means pressure rose; a negative number means it fell. The 24-hour comparison gives a broader trend than the 3-hour reading.

How pressure changes can affect fishing

Think of pressure as a supporting clue, not a fish-activity meter. The direction and speed of change can help describe changing weather, but there is no pressure number that reliably switches fish on or off.

  • Rising pressure: Often follows a front. Conditions may be clearing, but fish can be less active for a period after a strong front. Try slower presentations and work likely holding areas.
  • Falling pressure: Can occur ahead of an approaching front. Fish may feed more actively during a stable, pre-front period, but worsening wind, rain, or rough water can outweigh that advantage.
  • Steady pressure: Usually means fewer pressure-related changes. Focus more on tide or water movement, wind, light, water temperature, bait, and the seasonal pattern.
  • Rapid change: A larger change over a short time is a stronger sign that weather is changing. Check the forecast and adjust for wind, storms, and safety before choosing a spot.

Reading the cards: The 3-hour change shows the recent short-term movement; 12 hours shows the half-day trend; 24 hours gives the broader daily change. Positive values mean pressure increased, negative values mean it decreased. For example, −1.7 hPa over 3 hours means pressure fell 1.7 hPa in that period. Compare the time periods together rather than treating one value as a rule.

Practical takeaway: Use pressure to anticipate weather transitions and decide whether to speed up or slow down your presentation. Do not choose a trip based on pressure alone, and do not assume a falling or rising reading guarantees better fishing.

Wind speed and direction

Wind can move bait, change surface conditions, and affect casting. In the surf, wind direction relative to the shoreline matters as much as speed. Strong onshore wind often makes the water rougher; sheltered shorelines may fish differently.

Wave height and period

Wave height estimates the vertical size of waves. Wave period is the time between waves. Longer-period waves can carry more energy even when wave height looks similar. These are modeled estimates, not a measurement at your exact cast.

Tides

Incoming tide is water moving toward higher tide; outgoing tide is moving toward lower tide. Current moving through cuts, passes, drains, and channels can concentrate bait and predators. The app uses the nearest available NOAA prediction station, which may not match the exact shoreline.

Water current direction and speed

Current is water moving horizontally. Speed tells you how fast it is moving; direction tells you where it is flowing toward. The Tide & Current Report uses Open-Meteo ocean-current model estimates (shown in mph and knots) at a broad grid of roughly 8 km. It is not a live measurement at your exact fishing spot, and local channels, guts, cuts, and shoreline seams can behave differently. NOAA tide predictions separately provide the expected high and low water times; a rising or falling tide does not guarantee the same current direction at every location.

Measured, modeled, and unverified map information

Measured means a sensor reported data at its gauge or station. Predicted means a station's tide/current forecast. Modeled means a computer model estimates conditions across an area. Unverified means the app has no reliable data for that exact patch of water. A nearby station does not prove the current at your fishing spot, and the app does not draw guessed arrows between stations.

Freshwater flow rate and gauge height

USGS streamflow (discharge) is the volume of water passing a point each second, usually shown in cubic feet per second (cfs). A higher cfs generally means more water moving through that river or creek, but it is not the speed of water at your exact bank spot. Gauge height is the water level at that monitoring station relative to its own reference gauge—not the depth of the lake. Lakes and ponds often have no current gauge; wind, inflowing creeks, dam releases, and outlets can still create localized movement. If no nearby gauge exists, the app will say so rather than invent a current reading.

Best Fishing Windows

The app compares forecast wind, gusts, rain chance, temperature, pressure trend, and—when available—surf waves. In saltwater mode, NOAA tide timing is also considered when predictions are available. Scores rank forecast periods against one another; they are not catch probabilities.
